RESEARCH // PROCESS LOG

~ Notes on making, before and alongside the playable files stored in /creations.

A lot of early-2000s browser and sixth-generation console games shaped how motion, friction, camera framing, and HUD legibility felt—yet many of them sit behind deprecated plugins (Flash/Shockwave) or ageing hardware today. These prototypes test conversational "vibecoding" as a method of visual-reference reconstruction: starting from remembered mechanics and screenshots, then iterating directly in single-file HTML and Three.js until the physics feel right.

Build Note 01 // Artifact: creations/freekick3d.html

Free Kick 3D — Rebuilding Set-Piece Muscle Memory

Visual Reference & Lineage

How It Was Built (Vibecoding & Systems)

Built as a single standalone HTML file pairing a deliberately low-resolution WebGL viewport (320×240, nearest-neighbour filtered) with a crisp 3× 2D canvas HUD overlay. Rather than importing heavy 3D assets, players, goal netting, and the animated stadium crowd are generated procedurally in Three.js r128.

Under the hood, the iteration moved from simple ball arcs to coupled set-piece mechanics: Magnus spin curves, wind drift, a wall that may or may not jump, and a goalkeeper whose reaction speed scales with performance. A lightweight neural network stored in localStorage tracks outcomes across 30 pitch spots (20m, 24m, 28m arcs), paired with a Cross-Entropy Method (CEM) Coach solver that searches for wall-clearing trajectories on demand.

Build Note 02 // Artifact: creations/bloodorangegolf3d.html

Blood-Orange / Outlaw Golf 3D — Transferring the Engine

Visual Reference & Lineage

This second build carries the low-res pixel visual language of Free Kick 3D into a wider 16:9 terrain space, drawing on mid-2000s arcade golf presentation (slanted HUD plates, composure/tempo swing bars, and drivable buggy traversal) alongside a toggleable synthwave BLOOD DRAGON / CLASSIC palette.

How It Was Built (Vibecoding & Systems)

Instead of static heightmaps, a single analytic elevation function HT(x, z) drives the terrain mesh, ball bounce normals, green-read slope arrows, water bowls, buggy suspension/jumps, and camera ground clearance across 9 deterministic holes. Zero-allocation typed-array buffers hold trajectory previews and multi-camera replays, while a second WebGL scissor viewport renders a live picture-in-picture landing camera.

Pacing is folded into the control loop: waiting on the green settles the composure ring (slowing the swing meter and earning mulligan tokens), while rushing speeds the bar up. Between shots, the camera hands over to a drift-physics golf buggy with an optional orb-collecting Adventure Drive.
